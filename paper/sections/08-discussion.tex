\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main result is an explicit low-dimensional solution inside an existing generalized-D-stability framework. The distinction matters for both novelty and interpretation. Generalized multiplicative region stability is classical in concept and was systematized by \citet{Kushel2019}; for conic regions and their complements, \citet{KushelPavani2022} give an abstract forbidden-boundary characterization under all positive diagonal multipliers. Theorem~\ref{thm:C10} does not replace that framework. It solves its real \(3\times3\) Matignon instance explicitly on the full-dimensional strict-\(P(-A)\) stratum.

Recent classical work continues to develop scalable principal-minor tests rather than exact low-dimensional angular elimination. In particular, Kushel's 2026 recursive determinantal framework gives a hierarchy of sufficient D-stability conditions for arbitrary dimension \citep{Kushel2026}. This reinforces the distinction between scalable sufficient testing in general dimension and the exact low-dimensional Matignon boundary studied here.

The comparison with Cain is especially sharp. At \(\alpha=1\), the Matignon region becomes the open left half-plane and
\[
T_\alpha(\beta)\to T_1(\beta)
=
\left(
\sqrt{\beta_{12}}+
\sqrt{\beta_{13}}+
\sqrt{\beta_{23}}
\right)^2.
\]
For every \(2/3<\alpha<1\),
\[
T_\alpha(\beta)>T_1(\beta),
\]
so the fractional problem is an exact outward deformation of the classical D-stability boundary rather than an unrelated sufficient criterion.

The comparison with Bahl--Cain is complementary. Inertia records how many eigenvalues lie in each open half-plane and on the imaginary axis. The Matignon problem requires strictly finer angular information because, for \(\alpha<1\), a nonempty sliver of the right half-plane is stable. Hence a fixed-inertia theorem cannot determine membership in \(\mathcal F_\alpha^{(3)}\).

\subsection{Why dimension three is special}

The projective orbit reduction of Lemma~\ref{lem:simplex-reduction} exists in every dimension. What is special in dimension three is the combination of two facts.

First, Theorem~\ref{thm:C09} shows that \(n=3\) is the first dimension in which
\[
\mathcal F_\alpha^{(n)}
\setminus
\mathcal D_H^{(n)}
\]
has nonempty full-dimensional interior. In dimension two, purely fractional D-stability is confined to a codimension-two set.

Second, after orbit normalization the characteristic polynomial is cubic:
\[
z^3+z^2+B_\beta(x)z+\kappa x_1x_2x_3.
\]
A crossing of a Matignon ray can therefore be solved explicitly and reduced to one scalar boundary function \(h_\alpha\). For \(n=4\), the normalized polynomial becomes
\[
z^4+z^3+B_2(x)z^2+B_3(x)z+B_4(x),
\]
with eleven nontrivial principal-minor invariants and a three-dimensional simplex. The orbit geometry is still explicit, but the quartic angular boundary depends on more than one normalized coefficient and no analogue of the scalar \(T_\alpha(\beta)\) has yet been obtained.

Exploratory computations suggest open genuinely fractional \(4\times4\) families beyond a single cycle, including two three-cycles sharing an edge. Those observations are intentionally excluded from the theorem claims here. They motivate a separate higher-dimensional problem rather than an expansion of the present manuscript.

\subsection{Robustness versus lower-dimensional boundary strata}

The exact theorem is stated on the strict-\(P(-A)\) stratum because Corollary~\ref{cor:strictP-interior} shows that every full-dimensional interior point must lie there. This is enough to characterize the robust class and to prove Theorem~\ref{thm:C09}. It does not classify every possible lower-dimensional boundary stratum of \(\mathcal F_\alpha^{(3)}\).

This distinction parallels classical work on the interior and strong forms of D-stability \citep{Hartfiel1980,Abed1986}. The present result concerns the open, perturbationally persistent fractional class. Matrices with vanishing signed principal minors may still exhibit special fractional stability, just as the \(2\times2\) purely imaginary family in Section~\ref{sec:dimension-two} does, but those boundary phenomena are not needed for the full-dimensional theory.

\subsection{Interpretation of the fractional band}

The inequality
\[
T_1(\beta)<\kappa<T_\alpha(\beta)
\]
should not be read merely as a statement that memory stabilizes an unstable matrix. It is stronger in two ways.

First, the right-hand inequality is uniform over the entire positive diagonal orbit:
\[
\sigma(DA)\subset\Sigma_\alpha
\qquad
\forall D\succ0.
\]
Second, the left-hand inequality states that classical Hurwitz D-stability fails somewhere on that same orbit. The genuinely fractional band is therefore an orbit-level separation between two stability notions.

The width
\[
W_\alpha(\beta)
=
T_\alpha(\beta)-T_1(\beta)
\]
quantifies the size of that separation in the determinant coordinate. Theorem~\ref{thm:C14} gives
\[
W_\alpha(\beta)
=
C(\beta)(1-\alpha)
+
O((1-\alpha)^2)
\]
near integer order, while Theorem~\ref{thm:loworder} shows a universal \(K^{-3}\) expansion of the fractional threshold as \(\alpha\downarrow2/3\). Thus the theory resolves both ends of the high-order regime.

\subsection{Ecological scope}

For a positive generalized Lotka--Volterra equilibrium, Proposition~\ref{prop:GLV-invariance} shows that abundance scaling does not change the classification. This gives a precise sense in which the fractional D-stability property is structural: it depends on normalized interaction feedback rather than on the particular positive equilibrium abundance vector.

The loop variables in Section~\ref{sec:ecology} should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Principal-minor/cycle decompositions are classical \citep{Kinkhabwala2015}; our new content is the exact fractional orbit boundary expressed in those coordinates. Moreover, Corollary~\ref{cor:sensitivity} and its \(g_{ij}\) form are partial derivatives in invariant space. A biological parameter change generally moves several invariants simultaneously, so the sign of one partial derivative is not by itself a causal prediction for an intervention.

\subsection{Limitations}

The present theory has five deliberate boundaries.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Commensurate order.}
The Matignon criterion used here applies to a common fractional order \(\alpha\). Multi-order fractional systems require a different spectral formulation \citep{BrandiburGarrappaKaslik2021}.

\item \textbf{Real matrices.}
The principal-minor and ecological interpretations are developed for real interaction matrices.

\item \textbf{Full-dimensional classification.}
The strict-P theorem completely describes the interior but not every lower-dimensional boundary stratum.

\item \textbf{Dimension three.}
The general simplex reduction extends to higher dimension, but the exact quartic and higher angular geometry remains open.

\item \textbf{Local equilibrium stability.}
The GLV application concerns linearized asymptotic stability of a positive equilibrium. It does not by itself establish global persistence, feasibility, or nonlinear basin properties.
\end{enumerate}

These limitations are not defects in the exact three-dimensional theorem; they define the natural continuation of the programme.
