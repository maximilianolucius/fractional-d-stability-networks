\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have given an explicit exact solution of the positive-diagonal Matignon generalized-D-stability problem for real \(3\times3\) matrices on the full-dimensional strict-\(P(-A)\) stratum. The universal quantifier over all positive diagonal multipliers is eliminated in favor of four orbit invariants,
\[
(\beta_{12},\beta_{13},\beta_{23},\kappa),
\]
and the scalar threshold condition
\[
\kappa<T_\alpha(\beta).
\]

The resulting boundary is an exact fractional deformation of Cain's classical real-\(3\times3\) D-stability threshold. It is generated by a globally strictly convex two-dimensional problem, has a unique smooth optimizer, admits an explicit global parametrization, and is resolved asymptotically at both
\[
\alpha\to1^-
\]
and
\[
\alpha\downarrow2/3.
\]
The exact genuinely fractional band is
\[
T_1(\beta)<\kappa<T_\alpha(\beta),
\]
and every point of that band is realized by an actual real matrix.

Combining this result with the exact \(2\times2\) classification shows that dimension three is the smallest real dimension in which the difference
\[
\mathcal F_\alpha^{(n)}
\setminus
\mathcal D_H^{(n)}
\]
has nonempty full-dimensional interior for every \(0<\alpha<1\). This is the precise sense in which robust purely fractional positive-diagonal stabilization first appears in dimension three; it does not exclude lower-dimensional boundary examples in dimension two.

For positive equilibria of three-species generalized Lotka--Volterra systems, abundance scaling simply reparametrizes the same positive-diagonal orbit. The exact threshold can therefore be expressed in abundance-invariant reciprocal-pair and directed-cycle feedback coordinates, together with exact invariant-space sensitivities.

The general simplex reduction extends to arbitrary dimension, but the dimension-specific root geometry becomes substantially richer from \(n=4\) onward. The present paper deliberately stops at the point where the three-dimensional problem is exact, closed, and independently audited. Higher-dimensional shared-cycle families provide a natural continuation rather than an ingredient needed to support the current theorem.
